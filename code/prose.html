On {{refs.sighting_date.value|raw}} the Spanish Armada was sighted off the Lizard in Cornwall, and a chain of beacons was lit to spread the warning. That date is in the English calendar of the time; by the Gregorian calendar Spain already used, it was 29 July. In {{refs.poem_written.value|raw}} Thomas Babington Macaulay turned the scene into a poem, "The Armada", with fire leaping from hill to hill until "twelve fair counties saw the blaze on Malvern's lonely height".

The fires left their mark on the map. Many hills in Britain are still called Beacon Hill, or Something Beacon. So I asked a simple question of them: how fast could fire carry the news? I took every hill and summit in the Ordnance Survey's gazetteer with "beacon" in its name, {{n_sites|int}} in all, traced the line of sight between every pair, and lit a fire at the one nearest the Lizard.

The answer has two halves. The beacon names on today's map do not make a chain: from the Lizard, the fire runs out after {{main.reached_from_lizard|int}} hills, all in Cornwall. But the gaps are small. Add a fire to the highest ground of every 10 km square and the news could reach the edge of London in {{filled.50.targets.london.hops|int}} hops, which is {{filled.50.targets.london.minutes_fast|dur}} at a brisk five minutes a hop and {{filled.50.targets.london.minutes_slow|dur}} at a slow thirty.

02 · The names{{n_sites|int}} hills called beacon

I started from OS Open Names, the Ordnance Survey's free gazetteer, and kept every hill, mountain or spot height with "beacon" in its name, plus the headland called Fire Beacon Point in Cornwall. {{n_beacon_hill|int}} are plain Beacon Hill; {{n_x_beacon|int}} are a place name followed by Beacon, like Dunkery Beacon, Ditchling Beacon or Worcestershire Beacon on the Malverns. I added two named historic beacons from OpenStreetMap that the gazetteer does not carry, Pinhaw Beacon and Penhill Beacon, and left out woods, farms, coastal rocks, reservoirs, the Brecon Beacons range and every navigation mark. Wikidata's beacons all turned out to be harbour lights and sea marks, so none is used. {{n_excluded|int}} candidates were dropped in all, each with its reason in the data.

The sites are overwhelmingly English: {{n_by_country.England|int}} in England, {{n_by_country.Wales|int}} in Wales and {{n_by_country.Scotland|int}} in Scotland. Wales and Scotland had their own warning fires, but their hills mostly carry Welsh, Gaelic or Scots names, and the English word "beacon" is rare there. So this is really a study of England, with a few outliers.

A Beacon Hill is evidence that someone once lit, or planned to light, a fire there. It is not proof of 1588.

Warning fires were used in England long before 1588 and long after it, and hills were named and renamed over the centuries. Some Beacon Hills may be named for a later bonfire or celebration. Many real Armada beacons, meanwhile, stood on hills whose names never mention it. The names are a sample of where fires were lit at some time, not a register of the 1588 chain.

03 · The chain breaksA fire that never leaves Cornwall

Within 50 km, {{main.sees_any|int}} of the {{n_sites|int}} sites ({{main.share_sees_any|pct0}}) can see at least one other beacon site's fire. The typical site is linked to {{degree_median|int}} others, and the best connected, Blackborough Beacon in Devon and three hills in Somerset, to {{degree_max|int}}. Of all pairs within 50 km, {{share_pairs_visible_50|pct0}} are in line of sight, and the median sight line in use is {{median_link_km|f1}} km.

But joining them up gives not one network but {{main.n_components|int}}. The largest has {{main.giant|int}} sites ({{main.share_giant|pct0}}). It runs from east Cornwall and Dartmoor through Somerset, Dorset, Wiltshire and Hampshire, and north through Gloucestershire and the Malverns to the edge of the Black Country. Next come a north-eastern network of {{main.second|int}} sites in North Yorkshire, Durham and Northumberland, and a Pennine one reaching from Westmorland and Lancashire to West Yorkshire. {{main.isolated|int}} sites can see no other at all.

The Lizard is in a Cornish network of {{start_component_size|int}} sites. Lit at Roskruge Beacon, the fire reaches all of them within {{main.max_hops|int}} hops, and then stops. The nearest crossing into Devon would need a sight line of {{bridged.malvern.gaps.0.km|f1}} km, from a hill called The Beacon in mid Cornwall to Penn Beacon on Dartmoor, a little beyond my 50 km limit. Allow it, and the fire runs on through Devon and Somerset to Malvern in {{bridged.malvern.hops|int}} hops.

London is out of reach on any terms. The only beacon-named hill within {{zone_km|int}} km of London is Beacon Hill at Purfleet in Thurrock, just {{zone_detail.london.0.elev_m|f1}} m above sea level on the elevation tiles. Its own fire could be seen from one other beacon site, across the Thames in Medway, but from it no other beacon fire is in sight at any distance up to 150 km. York has no beacon-named hill within {{zone_km|int}} km at all, and neither has Berwick.

So the names alone do not make a chain. That is not a surprise, and it is not evidence that there was no chain: it shows how much of the 1588 system has left no trace in the names on today's map.

04 · Filling the gapsHow fast could fire carry the news?

To see how close the names come to a working chain, I added possible relay hills: one point on the highest ground of each 10 km square of the National Grid, {{filled.n_relays|int}} in all, over Great Britain up to roughly the Forth. These are not known beacon sites. They stand for the many beacons, watch hills and church towers that left no "beacon" in the gazetteer. Then I let the fire spread through the beacon sites and the relay hills together.

How long is a hop?

Nobody timed the 1588 beacons, and I found no reliable figure for how long one took to answer another. So I treat the time per hop as an assumption and show a range. At the quick end, five minutes: a watcher who is awake and looking the right way, with dry fuel ready to light. In the middle, fifteen minutes. At the slow end, thirty: the watcher has to notice the glow, make sure it is a beacon and not a house fire, and get a large fire going in the wind.

On those terms the news reaches the edge of London in {{filled.50.targets.london.minutes_fast|dur}}, {{filled.50.targets.london.minutes_central|dur}} or {{filled.50.targets.london.minutes_slow|dur}}. York takes {{filled.50.targets.york.minutes_fast|dur}} to {{filled.50.targets.york.minutes_slow|dur}}, and the Scottish border near Berwick {{filled.50.targets.berwick.minutes_fast|dur}} to {{filled.50.targets.berwick.minutes_slow|dur}}. With a fixed time per hop, the quickest route is simply the one with the fewest hops; real delays would vary from hill to hill and night to night.

06 · ChokepointsWhere one fire matters most

Some beacon sites sit on far more of the shortest routes than others. The measure is betweenness: the share of shortest routes between all pairs of beacon sites that pass through a site. Top of the list is Beacon Batch, the summit of Black Down, the highest hill in the Mendips, a range Macaulay names in the poem. Take it away and {{chokepoints.0.giant_lost|int}} sites drop out of the largest network. {{n_cut_vertices_giant|int}} sites in the largest network are cut points like this: lose one and the network splits.

07 · LimitsWhat this can and cannot say

Names are not history. A Beacon Hill tells you a fire was lit or planned there at some time. It does not date it, and many real 1588 beacons have no such name. The relay hills are a model of what the ground allowed, not a map of the missing beacons.

Seeing a line is not seeing a fire. A clear line of sight at night is necessary but not enough. Haze, rain, low cloud and the size of the fire all matter, and a fire 50 km away is a small spark. I used 50 km as the main limit, and show 30, 70 and 100 km below. Smoke by day carries differently again.

The ground model is coarse. The elevation tiles have pixels about 90 m across and smooth off summits: Worcestershire Beacon, {{refs.worcs_height_m.value|int}} m high, comes out at {{zone_detail.malvern.0.elev_m|int}} m. There are no trees or buildings in the model. The name points are moved to the highest ground within 250 m (a median shift of {{snap_median_m|int}} m), and I also ran everything from the original points.

The delays are assumptions. Five, fifteen and thirty minutes a hop are my choices, not measurements. The hop counts are the robust result; the times scale with whatever delay you prefer.

How the results change with the assumptions
Beacon names only. "Reached" counts the sites the Lizard fire reaches, including the first.
Summit: points moved to the highest ground within 250 m. Name point: the gazetteer point as given. A 10 m fire stands for a tall blaze on a platform or a column of smoke.

Allowing 70 km sight lines and a 10 m fire, the names alone reach Carlisle and Beacon Fell but still not London or York. Cutting the range to 30 km leaves the Lizard fire only {{sensitivity.0.reached|int}} sites.

How I did this

Sites. OS Open Names (OGL v3), every landform of type hill or mountain, or spot height, whose name contains "beacon", plus Fire Beacon Point; two named OpenStreetMap historic beacons (ODbL); duplicates within 1 km merged. Excluded candidates are listed with reasons.

Ground and sight lines. AWS Terrain Tiles (Mapzen/Tilezen) at zoom 10, about 90 m pixels, sampled every 45 m along each line with bilinear interpolation. A fire 5 m above the ground at one site is visible from a watcher 2 m above the ground at another if no ground, raised by the curve of the earth with standard refraction (k = 0.13), reaches the line between them. This is the same code as my study Within Sight, which checked it on synthetic ground.

Network. A fire passes from site A to site B when A's fire is visible from B. The fire starts at the beacon site nearest Lizard Point (OS Open Names) and spreads by fewest hops. London, York, Carlisle and Berwick count as reached when any lit site within {{zone_km|int}} km of their OS Open Names point is lit. Betweenness is computed on the undirected network of beacon names within 50 km.

Filled network. One relay point per 10 km square of the British National Grid south of northing 700 km, at the highest elevation sampled every 100 m; squares below 5 m are treated as sea. Sight lines among all {{filled.n_nodes|int}} points up to 50 km. The fewest-relay route gives each relay hill a cost of 1,000 hops, so it minimises relay hills first and hops second.

Time. Hops times an assumed delay of 5, 15 or 30 minutes. All numbers on this page come from the published results file.
